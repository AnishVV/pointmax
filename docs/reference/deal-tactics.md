# Deal Tactics: Mistake Fares, Transfer Bonuses & Higher-Risk Techniques

> Imported 2026-10-03 from the "Travel point maximizer" project (pasted by Anish into the "Import artifacts from earlier project" thread). Text verbatim.

**Volatility: MEDIUM** for the mechanics; **HIGH** for the current-offer examples (timestamped to September 2026 — treat as illustrative).

## Mistake / error fares

How they happen: data-entry errors (e.g. €150 entered instead of €1,500), bad currency conversion, missing fuel surcharges, or competing-airline pricing algorithms undercutting each other.

Where found: flexible-date search alerts, FlyerTalk/Reddit deal threads, dedicated deal-alert accounts and newsletters.

Best practice if found:

* Book immediately, with a credit card (dispute protection).
* Don't call the airline to ask questions — this speeds up correction.
* Don't count on the trip until ~72 hours pass without a cancellation notice.
* Airlines can legally cancel provably erroneous fares in most jurisdictions — no guaranteed honor.

## Transfer bonuses

Banks (Amex Membership Rewards, Chase Ultimate Rewards, Citi ThankYou, Capital One) periodically offer bonused transfers — commonly 20–30%, occasionally higher — to a specific airline/hotel partner for a limited window.

Example snapshot (September 2026, illustrative only — re-check current offers):

* Amex Membership Rewards → British Airways/Iberia/Aer Lingus Avios: 30% bonus
* Chase Ultimate Rewards → Air Canada Aeroplan: 20% bonus
* Chase Ultimate Rewards → Marriott Bonvoy: 70% bonus
* Citi ThankYou → Japan Airlines: 30% bonus

Strategy: hold flexible bank points by default; only transfer when (a) you have a specific redemption ready to book and (b) a bonus is live for that partner. Transfers are one-way — points sitting in an airline account lose flexibility.

## Fuel dumping / throwaway ticketing (higher risk)

Fuel surcharges vary by the specific city-pair/routing, not just the operating carrier. Adding an extra, unused final segment routed through a lower-surcharge market can cut the surcharge on the segments actually flown.

Examples:

* Toronto–Frankfurt–Washington, flying only to Frankfurt: surcharge dropped from $776 to $623.
* US–Tokyo routed through Hong Kong (a low-surcharge market): surcharge dropped from $259 to $104.

Real risks:

* Only works reliably carry-on only — checked bags route to the final ticketed destination, not where you actually deplane.
* Airlines can flag or close frequent flyer accounts for repeated throwaway ticketing.
* Not something to automate or repeat often.

## Hidden-city ("skiplagged") ticketing (higher risk)

Booking an itinerary where your real destination is the layover city (because that priced cheaper than a direct ticket there), then not flying the final leg.

* Generally not illegal — a U.S. court ruling has upheld it as legal.
* Does violate most airlines' contracts of carriage. Consequences can include forfeited return segments, lost miles/status, or account bans if done repeatedly.
* Best treated as a manual, occasional cash-fare trick — not a feature to surface as a first-class search result.

## Tool implication

Fuel dumping and hidden-city ticketing are cash-fare/surcharge tricks, not core to an award-search mission. Worth recognizing if a result looks anomalously cheap, but out of scope to automate given the account-risk profile — flag as "manual/advanced, use sparingly" rather than auto-booking.
